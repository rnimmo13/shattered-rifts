PHASE 1 SCOPE
Create exactly:
• One polished idle animation.
• Three basic, reusable action animations selected from the class’s actual gameplay.

Select distinct actions that provide broad coverage. Typically:
1. The class’s primary offensive action.
2. Its most representative defensive or evasive action.
3. A second common action with meaningfully different movement.

Do not force every class into sword attacks and shield blocks. Adapt to the class’s actual weapons and mechanics. For example, a knight might need a basic cut, a shield block, and a sweeping attack—but confirm that choice from the game folder.

Avoid elaborate finishers, rare signature abilities, death animations, full locomotion sets, or large collections of card-specific clips in Phase 1. Record useful additions for Phase 2 without building them.

VISUAL QUALITY TARGET
Target the dimensionality, expressive posing, and polish associated with Slay the Spire 2 while preserving my original character’s identity. This is a visual-quality reference, not an assumption about that game’s production pipeline.

Create crisp illustrated 2D artwork with deliberate linework, controlled cel-shading, coherent perspective, convincing volume, and clear material separation.

The character must feel like a carefully drawn figure occupying space. Avoid photorealism, plastic-looking 3D rendering, AI smearing, inconsistent detail, and style changes between frames.

CHARACTER IDENTITY AND PROPORTIONS
Preserve recognizable armor, clothing, palette, heraldry, face or helmet, equipment, and personality from the reference.

Correct anatomical weaknesses without unnecessarily redesigning the character.

For an adult human knight, use approximately 6.5–7-head proportions unless the reference deliberately establishes another proportion system. Give the character sufficient leg length, a substantial torso, believable arm reach, and connected shoulders and hips.

Respect other classes’ species, physique, and intended stylization. Do not give every character the same body.

Keep proportions consistent across every pose. Default to a three-quarter combat view facing right unless the game’s current presentation establishes another requirement.

HANDS, WEAPONS, AND EQUIPMENT — NONNEGOTIABLE
Establish correct grips and attachments before animating.

For a sword:
• Four fingers wrap around the handle with an opposing thumb.
• Knuckles and wrist orientation must be anatomically coherent.
• The crossguard sits above the fist; the pommel sits below it.
• Blade, guard, grip, and pommel remain one rigid object.
• The handle stays seated in the same hand throughout the action.

For a shield:
• Establish a plausible grip and/or strap system appropriate to its design.
• Connect it mechanically to the hand and supporting forearm.
• Preserve that attachment through movement, contact, recoil, and recovery.
• Place it between the character and the incoming attack when blocking.

Apply equally convincing contacts to all other weapons, casting implements, and props.

No sliding handles, disconnected wrists, changing fingers, bending blades, floating shields, or unexplained equipment changes. Never hide errors behind effects, blur, armor, or camera movement.

FUNCTIONAL MOVEMENT
Respect equipment mass, balance, leverage, inertia, and stopping distance. Do not make every weapon excessively heavy or effortless.

Coordinate feet, weight transfer, hips, torso, shoulders, elbows, wrists, and equipment in a sequence appropriate to each technique.

For historically grounded combat, use plausible guards, cuts, thrusts, parries, and recovery positions compatible with the actual equipment. Fantasy actions must still preserve anatomy, balance, and readable cause and effect.

Keep the center of mass supported. Feet must plant, pivot, step, and lift intentionally.

Avoid skating feet, floating bodies, collapsing knees, impossible shoulder rotations, arms passing through the torso, and decorative spins that undermine the action.

Each attack must communicate preparation, acceleration, a clear weapon or casting path, a defined release/contact moment, follow-through, and controlled recovery.

Each defensive action must communicate interception or avoidance, appropriate bracing or displacement, a believable reaction to force, and recovery.

ART AND RIG CONSTRUCTION
Build and deliver a genuine editable articulated 2D skeletal rig with:
• Carefully separated transparent artwork.
• Painted overlap beneath joints.
• Anatomically correct pivots and hierarchy.
• Rigid weapons and plate armor.
• Controlled deformation for flexible materials.
• Independent thigh, shin, and foot controls.
• Stable ground contacts.
• Correct depth ordering and equipment attachments.
• Alternate drawings wherever rotation alone cannot preserve anatomy or perspective.

Preserve volume through movement. Use alternate drawings or controlled foreshortening instead of stretching parts into impossible shapes.

Add restrained secondary motion to cloth, hair, straps, and capes. These elements must follow acceleration and settle naturally.

Do not build the character by stretching a flattened portrait or rotating rectangular image crops. Moving disconnected parts is not acceptable character animation.

SKELETON, CONTROLS, AND EDITABLE SOURCE — REQUIRED
Create an explicit rest/bind pose, consistent coordinate system, documented units and scale, and a stable ground origin. Keep the skeleton, visual attachments, animator controls, and animation tracks identifiable and editable.

For a human character, include an appropriate hierarchy covering:
• Root/world control and pelvis/center of mass.
• Torso/spine, chest, neck, and head.
• Left and right shoulder/clavicle, upper arm, forearm, and hand.
• Left and right thigh, shin, ankle/foot, and toe where needed.
• Weapon and shield attachment sockets parented to the correct hand or forearm.
• Additional cloth, cape, hair, or accessory controls only where the design needs them.

Adapt this anatomy for other species. Name left and right consistently from the character's perspective. Set pivots at anatomical joints and preserve plausible segment lengths. Establish elbow/knee bend directions and joint limits where supported; otherwise enforce them in the authored tracks and validate their motion.

Provide usable controls for root motion, torso rotation, gaze/head direction, each hand, and each foot. Use IK with explicit bend controls and planted-foot targets where supported, or an equivalent constraint/control solution that prevents foot sliding and preserves limb lengths. Use FK or controlled IK/FK switching where appropriate for attack arcs. Bake constraint results when the target runtime requires it, while retaining the editable authoring source.

Weapons and plate armor must remain rigid. Bind rigid artwork to the correct bones or attachments; use mesh weights only for materials that should deform. Prevent flexible deformation from bending a blade or stretching plate armor. Parent sword geometry to its grip socket and keep the fist seated around it. Bind the shield to its supporting forearm with an explicit grip/strap arrangement. Document attachment offsets and any intentional attachment changes.

Provide individually named transparent artwork parts and a layered assembly source or equivalent editable attachment layout. Paint hidden joint overlap and complete occluded regions before rigging; do not simply erase the background and slice visible portrait rectangles. Supply alternate hands, limb views, torso/head angles, or equipment views wherever the required movement changes perspective. Include attachment swaps and draw-order keys in the animation data when needed.

Save actual authored keyframes, track interpolation/easing, constraints or baked results, attachment swaps, draw order, and event markers. The delivered files must contain enough data to reconstruct and edit the character and all four clips without relying on an inaccessible session or remote account.

ANIMATION AUTHORING — REQUIRED
Author exactly four named clips: idle and the three gameplay-selected actions. Each must animate the articulated character rather than moving the whole portrait. Idle should include restrained breathing and secondary motion while the feet remain planted. Action clips must coordinate lower-body support, hips, torso, shoulders, arms, hands, and equipment through preparation, contact/interception, follow-through, and recovery.

Use alternate drawings to preserve volume and perspective when bone rotation alone is insufficient. Do not use AI interpolation, morphing between unrelated poses, camera motion, crossfades, or repeated stills as a substitute for authored character movement. Rendered previews and sprite exports may supplement the rig but do not replace it.

Define each clip's duration, playback/loop behavior, reference guard, event times, and root-motion policy. Actions must recover into a compatible guard without reversing the attack. Resolve foot contacts and equipment attachments throughout the entire interval between keys, not only at selected poses.

TIMING AND GAMEPLAY
Use the game’s existing action timings and event conventions when they are clearly established. Do not confuse damage numbers, status durations, or turn counts with animation frames.

Where no animation timing exists, choose timing that supports readable, responsive combat. Do not force every action into the same duration.

For video previews, use approximately four seconds per action cycle when practical, including preparation and recovery. Keep the actual action timing appropriate to the move.

Keep camera perspective and character scale fixed. Frame the complete character and full equipment path with adequate margins.

Idle should loop seamlessly. Each action should begin and end in compatible idle/guard poses for clean transitions. Any demonstration loop must reset through believable recovery—not reversal, teleportation, crossfade, or morphing.

Expose useful animation events where supported, such as:
• Attack contact or projectile release.
• Defensive interception.
• Recovery completion.

VISUAL POLISH
The motion must read correctly with effects disabled.

Only after the underlying animation works, add restrained class-appropriate effects. Weapon trails must follow the actual blade path. Contact effects must originate at the contact point.

Use impact emphasis sparingly. Glowing arcs, particles, and screen shake must never substitute for correct anatomy or choreography.
